EXPLANATION

1. Let's denote Dana's walking speed as \( W \) mph.
2. According to the problem, her running speed \( R \) is four times her walking speed, so we have \( R = 4W \).
3. Her skipping speed \( S \) is half her running speed, which means \( S = \frac{R}{2} = \frac{4W}{2} = 2W \).
4. We know from the problem statement that her skipping speed \( S \) is given as 3 mph. Therefore, we can set up the equation \( 2W = 3 \), solving for \( W \):
   \[
   2W = 3 \\
   W = \frac{3}{2} = 1.5 \text{ mph}
   \]
5. Now, using \( W \), we can find her running speed \( R \):
   \[
   R = 4W = 4 \times 1.5 = 6 \text{ mph}
   \]

6. Next, let's calculate the distances she covers during each activity:
   - Walking distance: \( D_{walk} = W \times T = 1.5 \times 6 = 9 \text{ miles} \)
   - Running distance: \( D_{run} = R \times T = 6 \times 6 = 36 \text{ miles} \)

7. She spends one-third of the total time (six hours) running and two-thirds walking:
   - Time spent running: \( T_{run} = \frac{1}{3} \times 6 = 2 \text{ hours} \)
   - Time spent walking: \( T_{walk} = \frac{2}{3} \times 6 = 4 \text{ hours} \)

8. Now, we sum the distances covered during these activities to get the total distance traveled:
   \[
   D_{total} = D_{walk} + D_{run} = 9 + 36 = 45 \text{ miles}
   \]

Therefore, the total distance Dana can travel in six hours is 45 miles.

The answer is: 45
