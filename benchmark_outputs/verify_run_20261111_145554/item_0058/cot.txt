Explanation:

Premise 1: A wooden bridge can carry no more than 5000 pounds.

Premise 2: A delivery truck filled with identical boxes, each weighing 15 pounds, will pass over the bridge.
  
Premise 3: The combined weight of the driver and the empty truck is 3755 pounds.

From Premise 1, we know that the total allowable weight for the entire truck (driver + boxes) is at most 5000 pounds.

Let's denote:
- \( W_d \) as the weight of the driver,
- \( W_b \) as the total weight of all boxes,

We have two equations based on Premises 2 and 3:

1. \( W_d + W_b = 5000 \)
2. \( W_d + W_b = 3755 \)

Subtracting these two equations gives us:

\( W_d - W_d + W_b - W_b = 5000 - 3755 \)
\( 0 = 1245 \)

This equation simplifies to zero, indicating that there must be some error or inconsistency because it results in a false statement when trying to subtract them directly. This suggests that either the driver's weight or the total box weight was misstated or misunderstood in our initial assumptions.

However, since both premises seem logically consistent and correctly state the conditions under which the bridge can safely handle loads, let’s re-evaluate if any additional constraints were overlooked.

Given the actual weights provided:
- Driver weight \( W_d \approx 3755 / 15 \) pounds ≈ 250.33 pounds.
- Total allowed load \( W_{total} \leq 5000 \).

If we assume the driver weighs approximately 250 pounds, then the remaining weight capacity for the boxes would be:

\( W_b = W_{total} - W_d \)
\( W_b = 5000 - 250 \)
\( W_b = 4750 \text{ pounds} \)

Now, we need to find how many boxes weigh 15 pounds each can fit into this 4750-pound limit:

\( \frac{W_b}{15} = \frac{4750}{15} \)
\( \frac{4750}{15} \approx 316.67 \)

Since we cannot have a fraction of a box, we round down to get the whole number of boxes:

Number of boxes = 316.

Thus, the maximum number of boxes which can be loaded onto the truck while not exceeding the bridge's weight limit is **316**.
